# Case Law Reference — CAM Audit

Organized by detection rule. All citations verified against primary research sources.

---

## Cross-Rule Cases (cited in multiple rules)

### Sheplers, Inc. v. Kabuto International (Nevada) Corp.
63 F. Supp. 2d 1306 (D. Kan. 1999)
**Rules:** 1, 2, 3, 4, 11

**Facts:** Shopping center tenant challenged CAM reconciliation that included management fees, legal bills, insurance proceeds, tenant charge-backs, and utility expenses unrelated to common areas. Lease stated CAM "shall specifically exclude all costs associated with the leasing activity in the shopping center and all capital expenditures."

**Holding:** Court excluded all challenged charges. Found the landlord's testimony that 100% of management costs were CAM-related "impossible to believe." Court applied the lease's express exclusion list literally. Also rejected both on-site and off-site management fees where landlord failed to document their relationship to actual CAM activities.

**Why it matters:** The leading case for express exclusion list enforcement. Demonstrates that courts apply exclusions literally and are skeptical of overbroad landlord claims.

---

### South Towne Centre, Inc. v. Burlington Coat Factory Warehouse of Dayton, Inc.
Ohio Court of Appeals, 1995
**Rules:** 1, 2, 11

**Facts:** Tenant challenged whether a shopping center sign was a recoverable CAM cost. The lease did not specifically list the signage expense among chargeable CAM costs. Extrinsic evidence from both parties failed to resolve the ambiguity.

**Holding:** Court applied strict construction against the drafter (landlord). When ambiguity remains after examining extrinsic evidence, the *contra proferentem* doctrine controls.

**Why it matters:** Establishes the tie-goes-to-the-tenant rule in CAM disputes. Any unlisted expense is non-recoverable under strictly drafted exclusion lists.

---

### Tin Tin Corp. v. Pacific Rim Park, LLC
170 Cal.App.4th 1220 (Cal. Ct. App. 2009)
**Rules:** 1, 9, 10, 12

**Facts:** 12 commercial tenants at a shopping center alleged the landlord unfairly charged them the cost of its LLC taxes and fees through CAM. The lease defined "Common Area Operating Expenses" broadly with examples including utilities, property management, "Real Property Taxes," and insurance premiums. Trial court treated LLC taxes as fitting "Real Property Taxes."

**Holding:** Court of Appeal reversed based on tenants' argument. LLC taxes and entity-level fees are ownership costs, not property operations costs. Expenses benefiting only one tenant's space cannot be pooled in the common area expense pool.

**Why it matters:** Establishes the entity-level vs. property-level boundary. Even broadly worded CAM definitions do not capture the landlord's own business taxes and entity costs.

## Rule 3: Management Fee Cases

### Clear Lake Ctr., L.P. v. Garden Ridge, L.P.
416 S.W.3d 527 (Tex. App. Houston [14th Dist.] 2013)

**Facts:** Lease defined "Common Area Costs" to include a 7.5% supervisory allowance. After acquisition by a new owner, landlord began billing a separate "management fee" on top. The 7.5% supervisory fee was then applied on top of the management fees, creating a fee-on-fee. Management fees documented in the record: $58,259 (2003) through $84,014 (2009). The management agreement separately provided a 5% fee of monthly gross collections.

**Holding:** Documented the fee-on-fee as a lease violation. "Supervisory fee applied to the management fees" was impermissible double-dipping. Even where management fees are allowed, lease language can limit recoverability to common-area management rather than whole-property management.

**Why it matters:** The primary published example of fee-on-fee in reported case law. Provides concrete dollar figures and illustrates how ownership changes trigger management fee abuse.

---

### Johanneson's, Inc. v. Kraus-Anderson, Inc.
Minnesota Court of Appeals, 1999

**Facts:** From 1986-1995, landlord charged only actual maintenance costs. In 1996, landlord unilaterally began charging 5% of tenant's gross revenues as a management fee.

**Holding:** Court found the new fee impermissible based on the prior course of dealing. The decade-long practice of charging only actual costs established the contractual baseline.

**Why it matters:** Course of dealing is binding. Landlords cannot unilaterally introduce new fee structures mid-lease. Applies to reclassification disputes (Rule 13) as well.

---

### McDonald's Corp. v. Goler
560 N.W.2d 458 (Neb. 1997)

**Facts:** Lease required tenant to pay landlord fees paid to a management company in addition to an administrative fee of 15% of all common area costs. Court addressed whether the dual structure was enforceable.

**Holding:** Enforced the dual fee structure as written. Where the contract clearly supports layered fees, courts will enforce them.

**Why it matters:** Contrast case. Shows that fee-on-fee CAN be enforceable if the lease specifically provides for it. The presence of an explicit dual-fee clause defeats an audit challenge.

## Rule 4: Pro-Rata Share Cases

### Payless ShoeSource, Inc. v. Diana Joye, as Trustee of the Dena Trust
No. 2:12-cv-00517 (E.D. Cal. Feb. 5, 2014) [later vacated by settlement]

**Facts:** Lease formula stated tenant's pro-rata share as 3,580 SF / 114,679 SF (total GLA of the Development) = 3.12%. After parcelization, landlord began charging based on a much smaller denominator, asserting a 26.92% share. The shift from development-wide to building-level denominator produced an 8.6x increase in the tenant's percentage.

**Holding:** Court granted summary judgment for the landlord based on course of conduct and waiver arguments. However, the opinion was later vacated pursuant to settlement. The case is cited for the explicit denominator math and to illustrate how parcelization creates denominator manipulation opportunities.

**Why it matters:** Best published example of explicit denominator arithmetic. Demonstrates how a development-to-building denominator shift can radically increase the tenant's share. The vacatur does not eliminate its value as an analytical example.

---

### Accenture LLP v. CSDV-MN Limited Partnership
No. 06 C 1270 (N.D. Ill. Nov. 19, 2007)

**Facts:** Tenant challenged whether a parking garage should be included in the building's "rentable area" denominator. The lease defined "Tenant's Share" as tenant rentable area divided by total building rentable area. Including the parking garage would have made the tenant's stated percentage shares in the lease schedule inaccurate from inception.

**Holding:** "Rentable area" was unambiguous within the four corners of the lease and did not include the parking garage. Including it would have made the negotiated percentage shares wrong "at the outset."

**Why it matters:** Establishes the "would have been wrong at the outset" test for denominator disputes. If including an area in the denominator would have made the stated percentage in the lease wrong from day one, the area should be excluded.

## Rule 6: CAM Cap Cases

### Murray Hill Mews Owners Corp. v. Rio Restaurant Associates L.P.
Appellate Division, First Department, 2012 (New York)

**Facts:** CPI-based rent escalation dispute. Tenant argued for non-compounding approach; landlord applied increases to the prior year's already-increased rent (compounding). Tenant had paid the landlord's compounded method for years before objecting.

**Holding:** No ambiguity. Clause required using the changing (prior-year) rent figure for CPI increases. Course of dealing supported compounded method. Reversed Appellate Term and dismissed tenant's defense. A practitioner analysis explicitly frames this as "cumulative vs. non-cumulative" producing "exponentially greater" payments.

**Why it matters:** Although a rent escalation case (not labeled "CAM cap"), courts cite it for: (1) plain meaning of escalation math, (2) course of dealing evidence, and (3) the rule that long-term payment without objection creates precedent.

---

### K-Bay Plaza, LLC v. Kmart Corp.
Appellate Division, First Department, 2015 (New York)

**Facts:** Rent escalation dispute with internally inconsistent drafting: "10% cumulative increases every five years" vs. rent illustrations reflecting different arithmetic.

**Holding:** Applied New York accrual/limitations principles for "constant computational methodology" disputes. Granted defendant summary judgment. Constant application of a methodology for years may be time-barred even if incorrect.

**Why it matters:** Statute of limitations defense is real. Tenants who discover cap methodology errors must act promptly. Audit every year, not just when the lease ends.

---

## Rule 7: Base Year Cases

### Continental Cas. Co. v. Polk Bros., Inc.
457 N.E.2d 1271 (Ill. App. Ct.)

**Facts:** Addressed reasonable expectations of parties under operating expense provisions including base year treatment.

**Holding:** Courts look to the four corners of the lease to determine operating expense obligations. Established general principles for base year interpretation under Illinois law.

---

### Gailrach Realty Co. v. Eastern Analytical Labs, Inc.
246 A.D.2d 532 (N.Y. App. Div. 2d Dep't 1998)

**Facts:** Landlord did not provide required annual computation statements for years 1990-1993. Lease required these statements as a condition precedent to collecting additional rent.

**Holding:** Because the landlord failed to provide required annual statements, the tenant was NOT liable for additional rent for those years. The condition precedent was not satisfied.

**Why it matters:** Procedural protection works both ways. If landlords fail to comply with their own statement and audit obligations, they lose the right to collect. Important for understanding audit rights as mandatory, not permissive.

---

### Kramer Levin Naftalis & Frankel, LLP v. Metropolitan 919 3rd Ave., LLC
2004 NY Slip Op 24510 (Sup. Ct., N.Y. County Dec. 14, 2004)

**Facts:** Tenant sought declaration for a large rent credit based on base-year comparisons of taxes and operating expenses. The computational methodology had been consistently disclosed and applied since 1995.

**Holding:** Tenant's claim was time-barred under the six-year statute of limitations. The clock started when the methodology was first disclosed and applied, not when the tenant "discovered" the error.

**Why it matters:** Limitations defense applies to base year errors too. Tenants must audit base year within the applicable SOL window from when the statement was first issued.

## Rule 9: Insurance Cases

### London Trocadero (2015) LLP v. Picturehouse Cinemas Limited
[2025] EWHC 1247 (Ch) (England and Wales)

**Facts:** Tenant challenged insurance rent charges that included commissions retained/rebated by the landlord to itself.

**Holding:** Landlord was not entitled to recover insurance rent representing commissions rebated to itself. Repayment ordered.

**Why it matters:** Broker commission-sharing arrangements between landlords and insurers are not part of the "premium payable." Even in sophisticated commercial leases, the landlord cannot profit from the insurance pass-through mechanism.

---

### Parsons Mfg. Corp. v. Superior Court
203 Cal. Rptr. 419 (Cal. App. 1984)

**Facts:** Insurance obligation dispute between landlord and tenant in a commercial lease context.

**Holding:** Absent clear contrary language, the lease places on the lessor the burden of insuring the premises.

**Why it matters:** Default rule favors tenants on insurance burdens. Landlord must have explicit lease authority to pass through insurance costs.

---

## Rule 10: Tax Cases

### Thrifty Payless, Inc. v. Americana at Brand
Cal. Ct. App. 2013 (B242573)

**Facts:** Tenant at a Glendale, CA shopping center alleged pro-rata shares of property taxes, insurance, and CAM substantially exceeded pre-lease estimates. Landlord allegedly failed to allocate expenses appropriately between retail and non-retail portions. Prop 13 reassessment triggered by ownership change was passed through to tenants.

**Holding:** Decision recites allocation concepts separating non-retail expenses from retail tenant charges, and requiring allocation using "sound accounting and management principles." Key case for Prop 13 reassessment and allocation disputes.

---

### Target Corp. v. Township of Toms River
New Jersey Tax Court

**Facts:** Tenant responsible for paying significant property taxes challenged whether it had standing to independently pursue a tax appeal when the landlord had no incentive to do so.

**Holding:** Established a five-factor balancing test: lease duration, tenant's relationship to property, adequacy of representation, ability to pursue appeal, and landlord's relationship with taxing authority. Tenants paying significant property taxes have standing to file their own appeals.

**Why it matters:** Tenants are not powerless on tax appeals. Even if the lease is silent, tenants can file appeals independently.

---

### Village Supermarkets, Inc. v. Township of West Orange
106 N.J. 634 (1987)

**Facts:** Commercial tenant sought to file a property tax appeal.

**Holding:** The law, not the lease, allows tenants to file and prosecute tax appeals. Silence in the lease does not deprive the tenant of the right to mount its own appeal.

**Why it matters:** Lease silence does not block tenant tax appeals. This right is statutory, not contractual.

---

## Rule 7 / Rule 4 Overlap: Methodology Statutes of Limitations

Key New York cases establishing that the limitations clock starts when a methodology is first applied, not when the tenant discovers the error:

- *Goldman Copeland Associates, P.C. v. Goodstein Bros.* (App. Div., 1st Dep't, 2000) — tenant paid under consistent escalation formula without protest for years; later overcharge claim time-barred.
- *Citicorp North America, Inc. v. Fifth Ave. 58/59 Acquisition Co.* (N.Y. trial, 2009) — rejected delayed accrual based on later "discovery" arguments.

**Practical implication:** Tenants must audit within the applicable SOL from the date each annual reconciliation statement was issued. Most states: 3-6 years (written contract SOL). See `capveri-business-context` skill for state-by-state SOL table.

## Rule 12: Audit Rights / Record Access

### McClain v. Octagon Plaza, LLC
Court of Appeal, 2008 (California)

**Facts:** Tenant sought access to landlord's CAM records. Lease was silent on audit rights.

**Holding:** Tenants have an implied right to review landlord CAM records under the covenant of good faith and fair dealing, even if the lease is silent on audit rights.

**Why it matters:** "No audit right in the lease" is not a complete defense. Most jurisdictions recognize an implied right of inspection for expenses the tenant is obligated to pay.

## CapEx Classification Cases

### Kmart Corp. v. Cragmere Associates, LLC
U.S. District Court (M.D.N.C.), 2008 (magistrate recommendation)

**Facts:** Landlord included parking lot replacement in "Common Area Costs" (CAM-style pass-through). Lease did not define "capital expenditure." Court applied the classic test: repairs in the nature of replacements that arrest deterioration and appreciably prolong life are capital in character.

**Holding:** Parking lot replacement was a capital expenditure excluded from recoverable costs under the lease. Summary judgment recommended for tenant.

**Why it matters:** Leading U.S. CapEx-as-CAM tenant-win case. The "arrests deterioration and appreciably prolongs life" test is the standard in the absence of an explicit lease definition.

---

### RioCan Holdings Inc. v. Metro Ontario Real Estate Limited
Ontario Superior Court of Justice, 2012; affirmed Ontario Court of Appeal (2012 ONCA 839)

**Facts:** Landlord amortized a parking lot rehabilitation program and charged the tenant as additional rent. Lease excluded expenditures "of a capital nature" by accepted accounting practice.

**Holding:** Parking lot rehabilitation work was capital, excluded by lease wording. Tenant win.

**Why it matters:** Canadian case widely cited for the proposition that lease wording tying "capital" to "accepted accounting practice" or GAAP creates a broad exclusion that captures major rehabilitation projects.
