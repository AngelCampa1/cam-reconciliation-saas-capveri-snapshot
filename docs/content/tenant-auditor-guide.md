# What Tenant Auditors Look For: A Landlord's Defensive Playbook

The audit isn't coming. It's already running. When your year-end reconciliation reaches a
tenant's desk, firms like Baker Tilly and Springbord have automated tools that flag
discrepancies before a human reviews a single line. A billing error above 3-5% of recoverable
costs isn't just a refund â€” in most leases, it triggers the landlord's obligation to pay the
tenant's audit costs outright. That changes the math on what "close enough" really means.

---

## Why Tenant Auditors Are Coming For You

Tango Analytics' analysis of U.S. retail CAM reconciliations found material billing errors in
40% of statements reviewed. Springbord's research on recovery rates puts the average amount
recovered by tenant audit firms at 15-20% of total CAM billed â€” not a rounding error.

The error rate exists for a specific reason. Springbord puts it plainly: manual lease
abstraction takes four hours per document. A 30-building portfolio with 200 leases means
800 hours of manual work per reconciliation cycle. The mistakes aren't malicious. They're
arithmetic. Springbord's analysis of reconciliation findings shows the majority trace to
internal process failures: inconsistent expense classification, undocumented gross-up
methodology, and missing base year records.

Leasecake has documented when audits get triggered: lease renewals, property ownership
transfers, year-end NNN reconciliation letters, and corporate acquisitions. Those are not
random. They're the moments when a tenant's legal team reviews the lease language in detail
for the first time in years. That review surfaces questions. Those questions become audit
notifications.

There's one more factor. ASC 842, effective 2019, raised the stakes. Commercial tenants
must now recognize operating leases on their balance sheets as Right-of-Use (ROU) assets. An
inflated CAM bill doesn't just hit the income statement â€” it distorts the ROU asset
calculation, affecting financial ratios that matter to investors and lenders. That gives CFOs
a second reason to care about reconciliation accuracy beyond the dollar amount.

## The 7 Things They Check First

Auditors don't start randomly. Every firm runs a targeted sweep against the line items where
property management accounting systems fail most predictably.

**1. CapEx/OpEx misclassification**

This is the highest-yield finding. Replacing an entire roof membrane is capital. Patching
isolated leaks is operating. Installing a new chiller is capital. Seasonal HVAC servicing is
operating. Auditors apply IRS Publication 946 and GAAP depreciation standards to challenge
any improvement that extends useful life or increases asset value. They also check
amortization: even where a lease permits CapEx pass-through, landlords frequently expense the
full cost in year one rather than spreading it over the asset's useful life.

| System | Allowable Operating Expense | Capital Expenditure |
|---|---|---|
| Roofing | Patching leaks, annual inspection | Full tear-off and membrane replacement |
| HVAC | Filter changes, seasonal service | Full chiller or rooftop unit replacement |
| Paving | Pothole fill, restriping | Complete asphalt overlay |
| Interior | Painting between tenants | Full lobby renovation |

**2. Gross-up applied to fixed costs**

Gross-up provisions exist to normalize variable expenses during partial vacancy. Variable
costs â€” janitorial, trash removal, common area utilities â€” legitimately fluctuate with
occupancy. Fixed costs â€” property insurance, real estate taxes, exterior landscaping â€” do
not. Applying the gross-up multiplier to fixed costs generates unearned landlord income.

BDO and Springbord both flag this as the single most common reconciliation error. The math
exposes it quickly. In a building that's 50% occupied with $100,000 in variable costs, the
correct gross-up (to a 95% occupancy assumption) yields a tenant share of $9,500 on a 10%
lease. Any number above that on fixed costs is a direct audit objection.

**3. Management fee base inflation**

Most leases define the management fee as a percentage of "gross operating expenses" â€”
and then specify what's included in that base. The problem: landlords often calculate the
fee on a base that includes capital expenditures, property taxes, or insurance, all of which
are commonly excluded from the fee calculation in well-negotiated leases. Auditors pull the
exact lease language and cross-check it against the actual dollar figure used as the
calculation base in the accounting system.

There's also a double-billing pattern BDO documents specifically: a landlord charges a
percentage-based administrative fee for property management overhead while simultaneously
billing direct payroll, benefits, and office expenses for the same management staff as
separate CAM line items.

**4. Ownership expenses bleeding into CAM**

Baker Tilly calls this category "unallowed ownership expenses." The principle is simple:
CAM reimburses costs of operating the property, not costs of running the ownership entity.
Auditors screen for executive salaries, corporate overhead allocations, leasing commissions,
tenant improvement allowances, legal fees from entity-level disputes or refinancing, and
costs for regional managers who don't have direct daily responsibility for the specific
building.

These items end up in CAM pools because property management accounting systems rarely force
a clean separation between entity-level and property-level costs. The ledgers commingle. The
auditor traces it.

**5. Pro-rata denominator manipulation**

A tenant occupying 10,000 SF in a 100,000 SF building has a 10% pro-rata share â€” if the
denominator is Gross Leasable Area (GLA, total building SF). If the denominator shrinks to
Leased Area (only currently occupied SF), that same tenant's share spikes when vacancies
grow. When a 50,000 SF anchor vacates, the tenant jumps from 10% to 20% of a smaller base.

Auditors verify that the denominator matches the lease definition and stays static. They
pull rent rolls month by month, per Springbord's published checklist, to catch mid-year
changes that shift pro-rata shares without corresponding adjustments to other tenants.

**6. Base year baseline errors**

Gross leases use a base year to establish the expense floor â€” the tenant pays only for
increases above that year's actual costs. A low base year means a bigger delta every year
after. Auditors reconstruct the base year ledger to find two common problems.

First, if the building was partially vacant during the base year and the landlord didn't
gross-up base year variable expenses, the baseline is artificially deflated. Every subsequent
year looks like a bigger increase than it actually is. Second, if the landlord switched from
in-house maintenance to a third-party vendor after the base year, the cost spike looks like
an expense increase when it's really a methodological shift. Auditors demand normalized
comparisons.

**7. Utility double-billing and sub-meter markups**

High-consumption tenants â€” data centers, restaurants, 24-hour retailers â€” drive
disproportionate utility costs. When landlords don't bill them directly, that consumption
lands in the general CAM pool, subsidized by everyone. Auditors cross-reference general pool
utility invoices against any direct tenant billing to find duplicated costs.

After-hours HVAC is a related issue. Office leases typically charge tenants directly for
overtime cooling. If that revenue isn't credited back to the general utility pool, the
landlord collects twice: once from the tenant who requested it, once from all tenants
sharing the pool. Sub-meter rate markups get flagged too â€” many leases and state PUC
regulations prohibit administrative premiums on sub-metered electricity rates.

## How to Audit Yourself Before They Do

You don't need an audit firm. You need to run the same screens they run, before the
reconciliation goes out.

**Run the CapEx screen.** Pull every invoice over $5,000. Apply the IRS/GAAP test: does this
expense extend useful life or increase asset value? If yes, it's capital. Verify an
amortization schedule exists with a defensible useful life. A 3-year amortization on a roof
replacement will be challenged immediately.

**Validate your gross-up arithmetic.** Separate your variable costs (janitorial, trash,
utilities) from fixed costs (taxes, insurance, landscaping contracts). Re-run the gross-up
calculation on variable costs only. Check the multiplier against actual monthly occupancy
reports, not an estimate.

**Reconstruct the denominator.** Pull rent roll data from Yardi or MRI for each month of the
reconciliation year. Confirm the denominator in every tenant's calculation matches the GLA
definition in their lease, not occupied area.

**Audit the management fee base.** Find the exact lease language defining what costs are
included in the fee calculation base. Cross-check it against the actual figure your
accounting system used. If CapEx, taxes, or insurance appear in that base, flag it.

**Isolate ownership expenses.** Export your GL. Search for corporate overhead allocations,
leasing costs, legal fees, and above-property personnel costs. Any item hitting the CAM pool
needs a paper trail that establishes it as a recoverable property expense.

## Documentation That Stops Disputes Cold

A correct reconciliation that's poorly documented is harder to defend than a well-documented
one that's slightly off. Audit clauses give tenants 12-36 months to request records
retroactively. The documentation gap on closed years reopens disputes that should be settled.

| Document | What It Proves | Common Gap |
|---|---|---|
| Invoice-to-GL reconciliation | Every dollar has a source document | Invoices don't match GL entries |
| CapEx amortization schedule | Capital items are spread over useful life | Full expensing in year one |
| Monthly occupancy reports | Gross-up applied at correct occupancy level | Approximated, not actual |
| Vendor contracts | Distinguishes fixed vs. variable costs | "We always estimated it" |
| Management fee base calculation | Fee applied to correct expense subset | Calculated on total expenses |
| Property vs. entity cost allocation | Ownership expenses excluded | Commingled ledger |
