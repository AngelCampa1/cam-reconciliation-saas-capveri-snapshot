<Alert type="info" title="Quick Answer">
CAM reconciliation is the annual process property managers and landlords use to settle the difference between estimated and actual common area maintenance expenses. Monthly estimates are collected from tenants throughout the year. The year-end reconciliation statement determines whether tenants owe additional money or receive a credit. Errors in this process are the primary source of CAM disputes and uncollected revenue.
</Alert>

Use this page as the pre-statement checklist for CAM reconciliation work. If you need the primary explainer for the topic, start with the [CAM Reconciliation Guide](/cam-reconciliation-guide). This page is narrower: it is the control sheet for property managers who already know what reconciliation is and need to make sure the file is ready to issue.

## What Are Common Area Maintenance (CAM) Charges?

CAM charges are the tenant's proportionate share of operating expenses for maintaining common areas in commercial properties. These areas include lobbies, hallways, parking lots, landscaping, and shared facilities that benefit all tenants.

## How Does CAM Reconciliation Work?

<Steps>
  <Step title="Monthly Estimates">
    You pay estimated CAM charges monthly based on the landlord's budget projection.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Year-End Calculation">
    Landlord tallies actual operating expenses at year end.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Pro Rata Allocation">
    Your share is calculated based on your rentable square footage percentage.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Reconciliation Statement">
    Statement shows estimated vs. actual, with adjustment amount.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Settlement">
    You either pay the shortfall or receive a credit/refund for overpayment.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Why CAM Reconciliation Accuracy Matters

- CAM charges can be a material part of occupancy costs, so errors at this scale compound quickly
- Billing errors leave an average of $25,000/building/year in uncollected CAM charges [[Source]](/sources#operating-recovery-loss-3-5-percent)
- Overcharges go uncontested when no one reviews the math. Errors persist for years across every affected tenant
- Property managers who run systematic reconciliations face fewer formal disputes and tenant audits
- Proper tracking protects landlords from exposure when tenants invoke their audit rights

## Pre-Statement File Checklist

Before a reconciliation statement goes out, the file should be complete enough that another person can reproduce the answer without asking the property manager for context. At minimum, keep these records together:

- Final GL export for the reconciliation year, including account codes, descriptions, vendors, invoice dates, and amounts
- Approved annual CAM budget and monthly estimate schedule sent to tenants
- Current rent roll with tenant RSF, commencement dates, expiration dates, and excluded spaces
- Lease abstracts for every tenant billed in the statement
- Prior-year final reconciliation packet, including credits, true-up balances, and dispute notes
- Copies of invoices for large repairs, tax bills, insurance renewals, and management fee charges
- Occupancy support for any gross-up calculation
- Denominator support for the rentable square footage used in each pool

The point is not to make the file bigger. The point is to make it reviewable. If the statement cannot be traced from GL export to tenant balance, the reconciliation is not ready for delivery.

## Control Review Before Delivery

Use a short control review before statements are sent:

1. Recalculate every tenant's pro-rata share from the rent roll.
2. Compare the CAM pool to the GL export and identify excluded accounts.
3. Tie property tax and insurance pass-throughs to the original bills.
4. Check every gross-up input against occupancy support.
5. Compare capped charges to the lease cap clause, not to last year's spreadsheet.
6. Confirm credits from the prior reconciliation were applied.
7. Review unusual vendor spikes and large one-time repairs.
8. Save the packet in a location tenants can be shown during an audit.

This review is where most avoidable disputes are caught. A tenant may disagree with a lease interpretation, but a missing invoice, stale rent roll, or wrong denominator is a preventable control failure.
