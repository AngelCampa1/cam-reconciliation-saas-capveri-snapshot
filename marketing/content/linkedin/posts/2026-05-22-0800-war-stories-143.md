CAM overbilling does not require intent to create legal liability. That is the part of the legal framework that most property managers have not internalized.

The lease creates an obligation to charge what the lease permits. If the reconciliation statement charges more than the lease permits, that is a breach of contract. The cause. a spreadsheet error, a configuration setting that was never reviewed, a category that shouldn't be in the pool. does not change the legal outcome. The charge was wrong. The tenant is owed a refund.

Tenant audit firms consistently report material discrepancies in the majority of reconciliation statements they review. These are not primarily composed of intentional overcharges. They are primarily composed of errors that persisted because nobody ran a systematic check against the lease before sending the statement.

The most common causes of these errors:
Gross-up applied to fixed costs. Property taxes and insurance don't vary with occupancy. Including them in the gross-up pool inflates tenant charges in every below-threshold year. Cumulative cap bank miscalculation. The cap tracks year-over-year increases. An error in year two compounds into years three, four, and five. By the time an auditor reviews it, the over-cap charges may span the entire audit window. Double-coded vendor invoice. An HVAC repair gets entered twice in AP. The billing system catches one entry. Nobody checks whether the GL total matches what was actually paid to the vendor. Capital project billed as operating expense. A roof replacement coded to a maintenance account. Recoverable in the year it appears unless the lease excludes capital expenditures,. Most leases do, either explicitly or through the definition of recoverable expenses.

In California, SB 1103 added a multiplier. For qualifying small-business tenants, microenterprises with five or fewer employees, restaurants with ten or fewer, nonprofits with twenty or fewer. willful overbilling carries treble damages and attorney's fees. The definition of "willful" has not been tested in court. That ambiguity is not protective. It creates a zone of uncertainty in which any error that could be characterized as systematic may be worth three times as much to dispute.

The four steps that address the most common causes before statements go out: scrub the GL to remove non-recoverable expenses; separate fixed and variable pools before applying any gross-up; flag any expense category with year-over-year growth above 5-10%; and run the gross-up factor, denominator, cap type, and admin fee sequence in that order.

The majority of reconciliations with material errors share the same root causes. The four-step process catches most of them. Running it takes less time than responding to a single tenant dispute letter.
