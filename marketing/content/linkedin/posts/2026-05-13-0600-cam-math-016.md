The management fee clause in a commercial lease does more work than any other single sentence in the document. It determines not just the amount, but the base, the cap, the exclusions from the gross-up, and whether the fee is inside or outside the controllable expense cap. Getting it wrong in three different ways costs landlords money and creates disputes.

Here's the framework for reading the clause correctly.

Step 1: Determine the calculation base.
Management fees are typically calculated as a percentage of something. gross revenues, total operating expenses, or some defined subset. The lease specifies which. If the fee base includes categories the lease excludes from CAM recovery (such as leasing commissions or capital expenditures), the fee is inflated before the tenant even receives a bill. The fee should be calculated on the recoverable pool, not the gross GL total.

Step 2: Identify the percentage ceiling.
Most commercial leases cap the management fee at 2-6% of the defined base. Verify that the actual fee amount, as billed, doesn't exceed the ceiling. Affiliated manager relationships (where the property management company is a related party) create additional exposure: if the market rate for the service is 3% and the landlord charges 5% to an entity they own, tenant auditors treat the 2-point spread as non-recoverable overhead.

Step 3: Determine the fee type for gross-up purposes.
Percentage-based management fees are variable expenses. they scale with the gross revenues the landlord collects, which correlates with occupancy. Variable expenses are subject to the gross-up calculation when occupancy falls below the lease threshold. Fixed-dollar management fees are fixed costs and are never grossed up. Getting this classification wrong adds or subtracts thousands from the gross-up adjustment.

Step 4: Identify the cap structure for the fee.
Some leases include the management fee inside the controllable expense cap. Others exclude it entirely, treating it as a pass-through. Still others cap the fee separately at a specific percentage ceiling. The sequence matters: if the cap applies before the admin fee is added, the fee is calculated on a lower base. If the fee is added first and then the cap applied, the cap may be partially offset by the fee.

Step 5: Check for double-counting.
The most frequently missed error: management fees and admin fees both appearing in the CAM pool when the lease allows only one. Some leases permit a management fee as a percentage of revenues plus a separate administrative overhead charge. Most permit one or the other. Both showing up in the GL is an immediate audit finding.

The double-counting trap occurs because management fees often come from the property management agreement, not the lease. The PM agreement and the lease don't always agree on what's recoverable. The lease controls.
