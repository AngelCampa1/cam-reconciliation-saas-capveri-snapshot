A spreadsheet changed cell B47 from $142,000 to $0 in November. No log. No name. No reason. That change is now in arbitration.

A spreadsheet is not an audit trail. That statement sounds obvious, but its implications for CAM reconciliation are significant. A spreadsheet shows you the current state of a file. It does not show you who changed cell B47 from $142,000 to $0 in November, what they changed it from, or why. If the cell is wrong, you have no record of how it got that way.

This matters because tenant audit firms consistently report material discrepancies in the majority of CAM reconciliation statements they review. Not all of those errors are accidental. Some are corrections that were never documented. Some are adjustments that made sense at the time but look problematic two years later when a tenant's auditor asks for an explanation.

When the auditor asks, property managers search for documentation that often doesn't exist.

The practical problem with spreadsheet-based reconciliation has three parts.

First, version control is informal. Files get copied, renamed, emailed, and saved to shared drives. Which version of the reconciliation is authoritative? The one the controller emailed in February, or the one in the shared folder that was updated in March? Without version control, the answer is contested.

Second, there is no change log. When a number changes between the draft statement and the final statement, that change should be documented with a reason. In a spreadsheet workflow, it rarely is. When an auditor asks why the management fee base changed between two versions, the answer is frequently "I don't remember."

Third, retention is uneven. CAM disputes can surface up to three years after a reconciliation statement was issued, depending on the lease's audit window. Five-year retention is the standard recommendation. Most spreadsheet-based operations cannot produce a complete, verified reconciliation package for a property from three years ago.

The audit trail problem is not primarily a legal risk (though it is that). It is an operational risk. When an error is disputed, a property manager who cannot reconstruct the original calculation from documented inputs cannot defend the charge even if the charge was correct.

Documentation that exists only in someone's memory is not documentation.
