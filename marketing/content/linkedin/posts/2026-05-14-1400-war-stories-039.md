When a tenant audit firm sends its opening request, most property managers treat it as a documentation exercise. Gather the GL, send the invoices, answer questions as they come in.

That approach is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The opening request is also the moment that defines the trajectory of the dispute. Here is what auditors check, and what the response posture should be for each category.

The GL reconciliation. Auditors tie the CAM pool total on the reconciliation statement back to the GL. If the numbers don't match to the penny, that discrepancy becomes finding number one. Before sending anything, run the same tie-out internally. Unexplained variances between the statement. The GL are the most common opening finding, and the easiest to avoid.

Expense categorization. Auditors sort every GL account into three buckets: clearly recoverable, clearly excluded, and contested. The contested accounts. often labeled with vague descriptions like "maintenance" or "general services" get scrutinized against the lease's operating expense definition. For each account that could be challenged, have the invoice. The lease clause that supports inclusion ready before the request arrives.

Gross-up documentation. The auditor will ask for the occupancy percentage you used, the threshold in the lease, and whether the calculation was applied to variable expenses only. If you cannot produce a written calculation showing those three elements, expect a finding even if the math was right.

Pro-rata denominator. The auditor will verify the tenant's rentable square footage against the lease exhibit and the building's total denominator. A denominator that changed between years without a corresponding lease amendment is a standard challenge.

Cap calculation. If the lease has a CAM cap, the auditor will reconstruct the cap year by year. Over-the-cap charges that weren't caught in year two compound into year five. Preparing the cap history before the engagement begins prevents the auditor from framing the problem at its largest.

Settlement posture. In our experience reviewing CAM audit outcomes, settlements typically land well below the initial claim. Landlords who respond quickly with organized documentation consistently land at the lower end of that range. Landlords who are slow or disorganized provide the auditor with more time to find additional issues.

The first 72 hours after an audit request arrives matter more than any other part of the process.
