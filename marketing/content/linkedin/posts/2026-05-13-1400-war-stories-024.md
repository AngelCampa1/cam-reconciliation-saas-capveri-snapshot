A CAM dispute demand letter is not a generic legal document. The state where the property sits changes everything.

Florida requires a 30-day written notice to the landlord before a tenant can pursue any legal remedy for a billing dispute. That notice is a condition precedent. Skip it and the tenant's claim is procedurally barred, regardless of merit.

California under SB 1103 requires a specific demand sequence before treble damages apply to qualifying small-business tenants. Miss the sequence and the enhanced damages provision does not attach.

Most demand letters that arrive in property management offices were drafted from a template that ignores these differences. The tenant's attorney either doesn't know the local requirement or assumes the landlord won't notice.

What property managers should do when a demand letter arrives:
Check the state. Check whether the notice requirement was satisfied. If it wasn't, that is a threshold defense before anyone looks at whether the underlying billing was correct.

The billing may still need review. An invalid demand letter does not mean the charges are right. It means the tenant has to restart the procedural clock before they can enforce anything.

But the first 30 days after a demand letter arrives determine how expensive the dispute becomes. Responding without reading the letter carefully is how a manageable billing question turns into litigation.
