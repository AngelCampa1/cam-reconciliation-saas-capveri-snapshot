Most property managers think a CAM dispute starts when the tenant sends a letter. It doesn't.

The dispute starts when the tenant's CPA, controller, or advisor looks at the reconciliation statement and decides something needs explaining. The letter comes later, after they've already identified what they believe the problems are.

By the time the property manager receives the dispute request for six documents and four independent calculations, the tenant's side has usually already done a preliminary analysis. The request is not an opening question. It is a structured effort to gather the documentation that confirms what the preliminary analysis suggested.

This is why the advice to "respond to what's asked, not more" misses the point. The tenant's advisor is not asking open-ended questions. They have a list. They need the documentation that either supports or undermines that list. A complete response that covers all six document categories and shows all four calculations gives them what they need to finalize their analysis.

A partial response or a summary statement with a note that "charges are per the lease" does not answer their question. It creates a second round of requests and signals that the documentation may not exist.

The three-week timeline from receipt to substantive response that is common in property management operations is also the timeline that transforms a routine verification request into an escalated dispute. Most tenants and their advisors interpret slow responses as evidence of a documentation problem.

The dispute that arrives as a letter has been a concern for weeks. How quickly and completely the property manager responds determines whether it stays a concern or becomes a claim.
