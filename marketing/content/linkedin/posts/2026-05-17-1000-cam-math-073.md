Most landlords understand that a 5% CAM cap limits how much they can charge. Almost none of them track the cumulative bank that lets them exceed 5% in a high-expense year.

Here's the three-year scenario with a $100,000 base and a 5% cumulative cap.

Year 1: actual growth 2%. Cap ceiling: $105,000. Amount billed: $102,000. Bank: 3%.
Year 2: actual growth 3%. Cap ceiling: $110,250. Amount billed: $105,060. Bank: 5% (cumulative: 8%).
Year 3: actual growth 10%. Actual amount: $116,617. Bank available: 8%. So the landlord can pass through 13% (5% + 8%). Amount billed: $116,617. Bank: 0%.

Under a non-cumulative cap in Year 3, the landlord would have billed only $110,313. The cumulative structure recovered $6,304 more: deferred recovery of expenses the landlord legitimately incurred and the tenant contractually agreed to pay above the threshold.

The bank disappears in spreadsheet handoffs. When the reconciliation template resets each year without carrying the bank balance forward, the landlord loses the ability to use the capacity they accumulated. It's not an overcharge. It's a landlord leaving their own money on the table because the tracking system didn't preserve the balance.

Courts generally will not imply a cumulative structure when the lease is silent. If the lease says "5% annual cap" without specifying cumulative mechanics, most jurisdictions treat it as non-cumulative. Read the clause.
