The conventional view is that CAM reconciliation is an operations issue. Private equity diligence teams treat it as a valuation issue.

The math is straightforward. At a 6% cap rate, every $1 of annual NOI difference is worth $16.67 in asset value. A CAM recovery gap of $100,000 per year. expenses the lease permits to be billed but aren't being billed correctly. is $1.67 million in suppressed value. On a $40 million acquisition, that gap is material enough to affect pricing.

What diligence teams look for that operating teams often miss:

Recovery ratio trends across multiple years. A stable recovery ratio is evidence of a consistent process. A declining ratio that doesn't correspond to vacancy changes signals a calculation problem.

The lease-to-GL mapping. Can every line item in the CAM pool be traced to a lease clause that supports its inclusion? Expenses that have been in the pool for years without anyone checking the lease are a standard finding.

Gross-up configuration. Was the gross-up applied only to variable expenses? At the correct threshold? Using the occupancy date the lease specifies? These are not esoteric questions. they are the three parameters that determine whether the gross-up is correct.

Cap documentation. Is the cap bank history documented year by year? If the lease has a cumulative cap, can the history be reproduced from records?

The point diligence teams reach is not "this portfolio has CAM problems." It is "the CAM problems in this portfolio are worth this much at this cap rate." That number comes off the price.

The portfolio manager who addresses these issues before the sale process begins captures the valuation benefit instead of conceding it.
