An expense stop and a base year are different mechanisms that produce similar-looking bills with different math. Getting them confused creates a three-year overbilling that compounds silently.

In a base-year lease, the tenant's obligation is: (Actual Expenses - Base Year Expenses) × Pro-Rata Share. The tenant absorbs the growth above the base.

In an expense stop lease, the structure is the same but the floor is a fixed dollar amount per square foot, not the actual expenses from a specific year. The tenant pays the excess above $X per SF regardless of what expenses were in any particular year.

The overbilling occurs when neither floor is normalized. If actual expenses were $400,000 in the base year and actual occupancy was 74%, the un-normalized base is $400,000. The normalized base (grossed up to 95%) is $400,000 / 0.74 × 0.95 = $542,857. Using the un-normalized base understates the floor by $142,857. Every tenant pays excess CAM on that $142,857 they shouldn't owe.

Over three years, at a pro-rata share of 10% and normal expense growth, that normalization error produces $79,571 in overbilling for a single tenant.

The check: confirm whether the base year was normalized before it was locked in. If the lease was signed while occupancy was below the gross-up threshold, the base year almost certainly wasn't normalized. That's the starting point for any base-year lease audit.
