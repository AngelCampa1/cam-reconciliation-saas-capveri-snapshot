A 5% cumulative cap on a $15,000 annual CAM charge. Actual increases: 2%, 3%, 2% over three years. Total banked capacity: 8%. In year four, expenses spike 12%. The landlord should be able to pass through 13% (5% + 8% banked). Instead, they apply only 5%.

That difference is $1,200 in year four alone. Multiplied across a 15-tenant building and compounded over the remaining lease term, the error is worth $15,750 or more in lost recovery that the landlord was contractually entitled to collect.

The cumulative bank disappears in one of three ways: a spreadsheet handoff between controllers that doesn't include the carryforward tab, an annual migration to a new reconciliation template that starts from scratch, or a property sale where the new owner inherits statements without the historical bank balance.

The math requires two numbers tracked separately every year: what was actually billed, and what the cap ceiling allowed. When billed is less than the ceiling, the difference carries forward. When billed exceeds the ceiling, the cap applies and the bank draws down.

Most reconciliation systems track one number: what was billed. The ceiling calculation and the bank balance have to live somewhere else. When that somewhere else doesn't survive the handoff, the bank is gone.

Track the ceiling as a separate field. Reconcile the bank balance every year before the new reconciliation runs. The lease is a contract. The bank is an entitlement the contract created.
