Here is a scenario that illustrates how CAM audit defense works in practice. And What separates outcomes that cost $50,000 from outcomes that cost $200,000.

A 25-tenant retail center receives an audit engagement notice from a firm representing three tenants. The engagement covers three years. The initial claim, stated in the opening letter, is approximately $180,000 across the three leases.

The property manager responds within 72 hours: acknowledgment of receipt, identification of the internal owner, timeline for documentation.

The property management team spends the next two weeks pulling documentation. They find two things during their own internal review before sending anything to the auditor.

First: the management fee for one tenant was calculated on gross revenue rather than net operating expenses. The lease specified net operating expenses. The error ran for two of the three years under review. Dollar impact: $28,000.

Second: a landscaping contract was included in the CAM pool for the center's common areas. For one of the three tenants, the lease excluded exterior maintenance improvements. The contract was partially allocable to improvements. not purely maintenance. Dollar impact: $11,000.

The property management team issues a proactive credit for both items before the auditor issues a formal finding. Total proactive credit: $39,000.

The auditor receives the documentation package, reviews it, and issues findings on three additional items: a minor pro-rata denominator discrepancy in year one, an admin fee that was double-charged for four months in year two, and a CapEx item coded as maintenance in year three.

All three findings are reviewed, confirmed, and credited. Additional credits: $22,000.

Total credits: $61,000. Initial claim: $180,000.

The $119,000 gap between the claim and the resolution is not primarily explained by the landlord winning arguments. It is explained by the auditor narrowing their scope once they received organized documentation and saw that the property manager had already self-identified and credited the largest legitimate errors.

The initial claim of $180,000 included speculative territory, items the auditor thought might be errors based on the summary statement. Documentation that addressed those items narrowed the scope. Proactive credits on confirmed errors removed the financial incentive to pursue secondary issues.

This is the playbook: respond quickly, produce complete documentation, identify and credit confirmed errors proactively, and defend contested items with lease citations. It does not require winning every argument. It requires limiting the scope of the engagement.
