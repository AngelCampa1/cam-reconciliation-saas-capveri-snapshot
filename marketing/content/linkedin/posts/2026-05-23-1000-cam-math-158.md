A $28,900 audit finding - three years of parking structure maintenance billed to office tenants whose leases excluded common areas below the second floor. The dispute started with one phrase: "common areas of the building."

The dispute letter came from a national office tenant on the fourth floor of a mixed-use property. They had just exercised their audit right for the prior three years. Their auditor's lead finding: the tenant had been paying for ground-floor retail parking lot maintenance since the building opened.

The building had 180,000 SF total. 60,000 SF of retail on floors 1-2, 120,000 SF of office on floors 3-8. Both use types shared a single CAM pool. The lease language referenced "common areas of the building," which the landlord had interpreted to mean all exterior and shared spaces, including the 400-space retail customer parking lot.

The tenant's lease said "common areas used by or for the benefit of tenants in the building." Their auditor's argument: office tenants enter through a separate entrance and never use the retail parking lot. It provides no benefit to the office tenants. It should not be in their pool.

Three years of parking lot maintenance, lighting, and resurfacing: $340,000. The tenant's pro-rata share of the office portion of the building: 8.5%. Their exposure: $28,900.

They won. Settlement was $26,500 after legal fees. the auditor found a few other items partially in their favor that offset some of the adjustment.

The core problem was structural. A mixed-use building with a single CAM pool requires every tenant's lease to contain explicit exclusions for costs related to the other use type. Without those exclusions, the lease language "common areas of the building" sweeps in everything. And Office tenants pay for retail infrastructure.

The better structure: separate pools by use type. Retail expenses in a retail pool with a retail denominator. Office expenses in an office pool with an office denominator. Shared building systems. elevators, central HVAC, fire suppression. allocated between the two pools by formula, typically by use-type SF ratio.

Setting this up in an ERP requires careful account mapping and denominator configuration, but it produces clean results that survive audits. The combined-pool approach with exclusions requires the exclusions to be negotiated into every lease and maintained in the billing system. And Any missing exclusion becomes a three-year liability.

The audit in this case could not have been prevented without addressing the lease language at signing. But the lesson for the portfolio going forward: when you acquire a mixed-use building, the first CAM task is confirming whether the pools are separated and whether the exclusions match what's actually in the pool.
