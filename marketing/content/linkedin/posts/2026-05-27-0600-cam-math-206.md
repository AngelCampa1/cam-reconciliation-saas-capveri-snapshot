The property had run at 78% occupancy for the better part of two years. The reconciliation controller had been grossing up variable expenses each year. janitorial, utilities, security, to the lease's 95% threshold. Standard application of a standard clause.

The tenant audit request came in October for the prior two reconciliation years. The auditor's first finding: the gross-up had been applied to the full CAM pool, not the variable portion.

The pool included $340,000 in property taxes and $87,000 in building insurance. Both were fixed expenses. They cost the same whether the building was 78% occupied or 100% occupied.

The correct gross-up base: $280,000 in variable expenses (cleaning, utilities, landscaping, security).

The incorrect base that had been used: $707,000. the full pool including property taxes and insurance.

Gross-up factor at 78% actual occupancy and 95% target: 0.95 / 0.78 = 1.218.

Applied to $707,000: adjusted pool = $860,926. Applied correctly to $280,000 variable only: $341,026 variable portion, plus $427,000 fixed untouched = $768,026 total.

The difference: $860,926 vs. $768,026 = $92,900 annual overbilling. The auditor had two years of this. Refund obligation: $185,800 before interest provisions.

The lease had gross-up language that was clear on the variable/fixed distinction. The controller had applied the formula correctly. The error was upstream: the variable expense pool was never separated from the fixed pool. The formula ran on the wrong base.

This is the most common gross-up error in commercial portfolios. The formula is well understood. The base calculation is not.

The fix requires two steps before the gross-up formula runs: categorize every GL account as fixed or variable, and pull the occupancy threshold from each tenant's lease. Fixed/variable categorization should happen at the account level, not the transaction level. Property taxes: fixed. Insurance: fixed. Janitorial: variable. Utilities: variable. Management fees: depends on whether they're percentage-based (variable in the sense of revenue-correlated, but typically excluded from gross-up because the circularity problem makes them non-grossable).

The categorization step takes 30 minutes once per property. Running the formula on the wrong base costs $90,000 per year.
