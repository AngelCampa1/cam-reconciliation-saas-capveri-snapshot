A $14,000 CAM charge hit a tenant's AP queue with no line-item detail. Three weeks and a broker call later, it became a formal dispute. Before anyone checked whether the math was even wrong.

Most CAM disputes start before anyone reviews the math. They start when the tenant's AP team sees a charge, doesn't understand it, and escalates.

No cover letter: the statement lands. AP sees the number, flags it, calls the facilities manager, who doesn't understand it, calls the property manager, who didn't prepare it, who calls the controller. Three weeks. Tenant frustration. Broker involvement. "Let's hire an auditor."

With a 300-word cover letter: the tenant reads what happened. Utility costs increased 12% due to the Q3 rate increase from the local utility. Insurance premiums increased 8% at renewal. The prior year included a one-time management credit that didn't recur. Payment due in 30 days per the lease.

The contrarian point: the cover letter is not courtesy. It's dispute prevention.

Four elements that prevent the escalation: the bottom-line number up front, the primary drivers of year-over-year change, the payment or credit timeline, and contact information for questions.

It takes 20 minutes to write after the reconciliation is done. It prevents the hours of tenant communication and dispute response that come from sending a statement with no context.

The statement provides the detail. The cover letter prevents the phone call about the detail.
