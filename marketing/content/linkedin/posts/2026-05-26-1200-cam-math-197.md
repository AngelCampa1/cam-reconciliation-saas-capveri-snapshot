The tenant had paid monthly CAM estimates of $8,400 for twelve months. The year-end reconciliation showed actual CAM of $89,600 for the year. True-up balance: $89,600 minus $100,800 paid = $11,200 credit owed by the landlord.

The property manager issued the statement and noted the credit. The lease said credits would be applied to the next month's CAM estimate. Standard. The tenant received the credit applied in April.

Three months later, the tenant's in-house accountant reviewed the reconciliation and found a problem. The gross-up calculation in the statement had applied a factor to property taxes. a fixed expense. Property taxes had been grossed up at a 95%/82% occupancy ratio, adding $7,200 to the pool. Under the lease, only variable expenses were subject to gross-up. Property taxes were fixed.

The tenant's actual credit should have been $18,400, not $11,200. The gross-up error had reduced their credit by $7,200.

They sent a formal dispute letter invoking their audit rights. The landlord reviewed the calculation, confirmed the error, and issued an amended statement with the additional $7,200 credit.

The dispute was resolved without counsel because the error was clear and the landlord responded within two weeks. But the process consumed roughly 12 hours of combined time on both sides, created a period of formal legal posture, and required amended documentation.

The specific error. applying gross-up to fixed expenses. is the second most common gross-up mistake after applying gross-up when the building is already at threshold occupancy. Property taxes and insurance do not vary with occupancy. They are fixed costs that cost the same whether the building is 60% occupied or 100% occupied. The gross-up formula applies only to variable expenses: cleaning, utilities, landscaping, security, costs that scale with the number of occupied tenants.

In this case, the gross-up clause in the lease explicitly limited the adjustment to "variable operating expenses." The controller who prepared the reconciliation had applied the formula to the full expense pool, not the variable portion.

This is an input error, not a formula error. The formula was correct. The base was wrong.

The audit-proof approach: before applying gross-up, split the expense pool into fixed and variable categories with documentation showing which account falls into which category. Apply the formula only to the variable pool. Keep that split documented in the reconciliation file. When the tenant asks why the gross-up number looks smaller than expected, you have the answer ready.
