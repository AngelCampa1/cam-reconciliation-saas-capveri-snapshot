A 50-property portfolio with a formal work order QA program had zero reconciliation QA. Three independent tenant audits in four years. The work orders were clean. The CAM statements were not.

The three-layer model that actually works: automated checks, peer review, and periodic sample audits.

Automated checks run on every reconciliation before it is finalized. They verify that the CAM pool total matches the GL, that the gross-up factor is appropriate for the actual occupancy, that property taxes and insurance are excluded from the variable pool, and that the pro-rata shares sum to 100% of the denominator. These checks take seconds and catch the errors that spreadsheet formulas miss.

Peer review means a second person reviews the reconciliation for a property they did not prepare. The reviewer is not checking arithmetic. They are asking whether the expense pool looks reasonable, whether any category grew more than expected without explanation,. Whether the documentation package would survive an audit request. A 30-minute review by someone who didn't prepare the statement catches a different category of errors than automated checks.

Sample audits mean that a small percentage of statements get independently recalculated from raw inputs before being sent. Three properties per quarter on a 50-property portfolio is enough to detect systematic errors before they compound.

The four quality metrics that indicate a program is working: error rate on completed statements, average time to resolve tenant disputes, percentage of statements with complete backup documentation, and recovery ratio trend across the portfolio.

The failure modes in reconciliation quality. gross-up on fixed expenses, stale occupancy data, excluded expenses creeping back into the pool. are predictable. A quality program is designed around the specific failure modes that are most common in a given operation.
