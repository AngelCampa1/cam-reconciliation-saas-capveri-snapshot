A common assumption worth questioning: the gross-up threshold misconfiguration that runs 3% over the actual occupancy stop is always a landlord-favorable error.

The implication is usually that landlords are overcharging tenants, intentionally or otherwise, and the tenant audit process exists to claw those overcharges back.

That's not how the data tends to break down in practice.

The error distribution in CAM reconciliation is not systematically directional. Errors run both ways. And some of the most common categories of error actually result in landlords leaving money on the table.

Here are three examples.

The gross-up threshold set too conservatively. A lease allows gross-up to 95% occupancy. The property accountant, not wanting to fight with tenants, sets the threshold at 90%. At 91% occupancy, no gross-up is applied even though the lease permits it. That's a legitimate recovery the landlord forfeited.

The exclusion list set too broadly. During lease negotiation, the tenant pushed for a wide exclusion list. The property manager agreed to some items but not all. At implementation, the accountant, working from notes rather than the signed exhibit, excluded more than was agreed. The landlord is subsidizing costs the tenant was supposed to share.

The cap base year set incorrectly. The lease allows increases above 5% annually to be capped. The property manager sets the cap base from Year 1 rather than the lease-specified base year. The tenant benefits from a more restrictive cap than the lease requires.

None of these errors involve intent. They're configuration decisions made under time pressure, by people working from summaries rather than the executed lease. The result is that the landlord's recovery rights under the lease are not being fully exercised.

This is part of the reason why an independent layer that reads the actual lease matters. It's not just about protecting tenants from overcharges. It's about ensuring landlords are actually recovering what the lease entitles them to recover.
