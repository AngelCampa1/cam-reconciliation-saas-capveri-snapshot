SLIDE 1:
The hidden cost of late reconciliation statements.
It is more than delayed cash.

SLIDE 2:
The cash flow impact
A 20-building portfolio with $15,000 average true-up per building holds $300,000 in receivables.
Sending statements 60 days late delays $300,000 in cash collection by two months.
At a 5% cost of capital, that is $2,500 in carrying cost.
That is the visible cost. The hidden costs are larger.

SLIDE 3:
Dispute rates rise with delay
True-up bills arriving 8 months after year-end face higher dispute rates than bills arriving within 90 days.
Tenants who budgeted for a Q1 true-up do not have budget authority for a surprise bill in August.
Their first instinct is to question the charges.
Late delivery does not cause errors. But it creates the adversarial dynamic that turns errors into disputes.

SLIDE 4:
The audit window extension
If your lease gives tenants 120 days to initiate an audit after receiving the statement, and you send statements 90 days late:
On-time delivery (March 31): audit window closes July 29.
Late delivery (June 30): audit window closes October 28.
Three additional months of audit exposure during Q4.

SLIDE 5:
Statutory penalties. California SB 1103
California requires landlords to respond to qualified commercial tenant documentation requests within 30 days.
Miss the 30-day window: treble damages are on the table.
The longer the statement delay, the longer the window during which a request can arrive without an organized response.

SLIDE 6:
Lease-specific forfeiture clauses
Some leases say: "If Landlord fails to deliver within 180 days of year-end, Landlord forfeits the right to collect any additional amounts for that year."
Others: "Estimates shall be deemed the final charges" if the statement is late.
Others: true-up amounts owed to the tenant accrue interest from day 90 until delivery.
These clauses exist. Check every lease before assuming late delivery has no cost.

SLIDE 7:
The permanent forfeiture threshold
Expenses older than 24-36 months become permanently uncollectable in most states, regardless of what the lease says (Holland & Knight).
California's SB 1103 sets an 18-month hard limit from the date of the tenant's written request.
A statement sent 30 months after year-end may be legally valid under the lease but economically unenforceable for the oldest expenses it contains.

SLIDE 8:
The 90-day standard is achievable.
The bottleneck is almost always late vendor invoices and slow GL close. not the calculation itself.
Solving those upstream problems is how you hit the window consistently.
