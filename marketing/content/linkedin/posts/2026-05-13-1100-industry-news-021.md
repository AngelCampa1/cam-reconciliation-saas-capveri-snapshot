California's SB 1103 has been in effect for 14 months. Here is what changed.

The law applies to small-business commercial tenants: microenterprises with five or fewer employees, restaurants with ten or fewer, nonprofits with twenty or fewer. For qualified tenants, willful overbilling now carries treble damages plus attorney's fees.

What landlords and property managers are reporting since January 2025:

Documentation requests from tenants increased. Tenants who never asked questions before are now requesting GL detail, vendor invoices, and expense allocation schedules. Some are doing it on legal advice. The documentation request itself is now a pre-litigation signal.

Audit firms gained bargaining power. Contingency audit firms serving small-business tenants have been more aggressive in California markets than anywhere else. The treble damages provision gives them a credible threat that changes settlement math.

"Willful" is still undefined by a court. Until there is case law, nobody knows where accidental misapplication ends and willful overbilling begins. That ambiguity cuts both ways. it disciplines landlords who know they have errors, and it arms plaintiffs' attorneys who want to characterize any error as willful.

What has not changed: the underlying billing process. Errors still happen at the same points. gross-up on fixed expenses, CapEx coded as OpEx, management fee calculated on the wrong base. SB 1103 raised the cost of those errors in California. It did not change where they occur.

The question for the 2026 reconciliation season is not whether tenants will find errors. It is whether the errors your statements contain are now worth ten times more to dispute.
