Property managers often assume tenant auditors are looking for fraud. They are not.

Fraud is rare, hard to prove, and not what makes tenant audits financially productive. The majority finding rate in tenant audits comes almost entirely from systematic errors that have nothing to do with intent. wrong inputs, misconfigured formulas, and categories that were never mapped against the lease.

The seven things auditors actually check: CapEx coded as OpEx, gross-up applied to fixed expenses, management fee calculated on the wrong base, expenses outside the lease definition, pro-rata share errors, cap violations, and base year anomalies.

None of these require dishonesty to occur. CapEx gets coded as OpEx because the person who entered the invoice didn't know the rule or didn't check. Gross-up gets applied to property taxes because the template was built before someone read the relevant lease clause. The management fee base gets wrong because the lease was amended and the billing configuration was not updated.

The auditor's advantage is not accusation. It is documentation. They produce a written comparison: what the lease permits, what was charged, and the dollar difference. That document is hard to argue with unless the property manager has already done the same comparison and can show it.

The most effective response to a tenant audit is not a better defense. It is a prior internal review that found the same issues and corrected them before the engagement notice arrived.

What auditors are looking for is available to property managers in advance. The seven checks are known. The question is when they get run.
