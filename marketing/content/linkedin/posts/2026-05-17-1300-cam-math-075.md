Two tenants in the same building. Same 5% cap. Same $80,000 base. After three years of identical actual expense growth, one owes $7,342 more than the other.

The difference is cumulative vs. non-cumulative.

Under a non-cumulative cap:
Year 1 cap ceiling: $84,000. Actual: $82,400. Billed: $82,400.
Year 2 cap ceiling: $86,520. Actual: $85,696. Billed: $85,696.
Year 3 cap ceiling: $89,816. Actual: $94,800. Cap triggered. Billed: $89,816.

Under a cumulative cap:
Same Year 1 and Year 2. same billing. But 1.6% + 1.9% = 3.5% banked.
Year 3 ceiling: $89,816 + 3.5% = $92,619. Billed: $92,619.

The $7,342 difference in total landlord recovery over three years (compared to what the non-cumulative landlord collected) is not a windfall. It's the carry-forward of legitimately deferred recovery in years when expenses grew below the cap ceiling.

For tenants: non-cumulative caps provide stronger protection. The landlord's recovery is capped at the annual ceiling and unused capacity disappears each year.

For landlords: cumulative caps preserve recovery capacity. A low-expense year builds a bank. A high-expense year draws from it.

Both use the same nominal cap percentage. They produce materially different results. The difference appears in the clause language, which most people signing a lease don't read carefully enough to notice.
