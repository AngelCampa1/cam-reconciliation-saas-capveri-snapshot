Tenant audit firms consistently find material billing errors in the reconciliation statements they review. A meaningful share of tenants report finding discrepancies on their own, without a professional auditor. That figure is harder to dismiss.

These are tenants who did not hire an audit firm, did not engage a CPA, and still found something wrong. They were doing their own math. checking whether the amount they were billed matched their understanding of the lease.

What they found, in aggregate, was that the answer was frequently no.

The four causes that account for the majority of those discrepancies:

Gross-up applied to fixed costs. Property taxes and insurance don't change with occupancy. Including them in the gross-up pool inflates tenant charges in every below-threshold year. This error is consistently the most common finding in tenant audit engagements.

Cumulative cap bank errors. The cap tracks year-over-year increases. An error in year two compounds into years three and four. By year five, the landlord may have been charging above-cap amounts for three consecutive years without anyone tracking the bank correctly.

Double-coded vendor invoices. An invoice entered twice in AP. The billing system processes both entries. Nobody compared the GL total to the invoices paid.

Capital projects coded as operating expenses. A roof replacement billed as maintenance. Recoverable as maintenance unless the lease excludes capital expenditures, which most leases do.

In California, the cost structure for these errors changed in January 2025. SB 1103 authorizes treble damages for willful overbilling against qualifying small-business tenants. The definition of "willful" has not been settled by courts. Until it is, any systematic error that has run for multiple years carries ambiguous exposure.

The four-step internal review process that catches these errors before statements are sent: scrub the GL for non-recoverable items; separate fixed and variable expense pools before applying the gross-up; flag expense categories with unusual year-over-year growth; run the gross-up factor, pro-rata denominator, cap type, and admin fee calculation in sequence.

A meaningful share of tenants found errors without professional help. The errors were in the statement when it was sent.
