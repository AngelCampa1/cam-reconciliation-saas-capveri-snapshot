A base-year lease and an NNN lease look similar from a tenant's perspective. Both pass through operating expenses. The difference is what the tenant pays in year one, and it compounds every year after that.

In a base-year lease, the tenant pays a base rent that includes the landlord's actual operating expenses from a specific year. The tenant's CAM obligation is only the excess above that base. In year one, assuming actual expenses equal the base year, the tenant pays zero in excess CAM.

In a pure NNN lease, the tenant pays a proportional share of all operating expenses from lease commencement, with no base year floor.

The compounding difference shows up when expenses grow. A base-year tenant at $210,000 actual CAM and a 2020 base year at $180,000 owes only $30,000 in excess. An NNN tenant in the same building owes the full $210,000 proportional share. The base year provides a floor that the NNN structure doesn't.

The trap for landlords: a base year set during an anomaly period locks in an artificially low floor. A lease signed in 2020 with a 2020 base year captures pandemic-era operating costs. 20-30% below normal because buildings were underoccupied. Every subsequent year of normal occupancy looks like a dramatic expense spike from the tenant's perspective, even when nothing unusual happened.

The fix: normalize the base year to 95% occupancy before it becomes the floor. BDO identified base-year anomalies as one of the highest-value findings in commercial lease audits because the error compounds across the entire remaining lease term.
