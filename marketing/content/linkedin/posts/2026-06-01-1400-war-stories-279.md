CAM demand letters arrive after the tenant has already done the preliminary work.

The six-document request is not exploratory. It is a structured evidence-gathering process from a team that has already identified the categories where they believe errors exist. They know what they are looking for before they ask for it.

The property manager who treats the letter as an opening question responds to what was asked. And No more. The property manager who understands the structure of a CAM dispute responds with a complete documentation package that addresses the most likely findings proactively.

Which approach works better is visible in outcome data. CAM audit settlements typically land well below the initial claim. The 25-point spread is almost entirely determined by documentation quality and response speed, not by the merits of the underlying billing.

The demand letter also contains two variables that most property managers miss: the state-specific procedural requirements and the framing of the initial claim.

On procedure: Florida requires a 30-day written notice as a condition precedent to any legal remedy. California's SB 1103 requires a specific sequence for treble damages to attach. If the letter doesn't satisfy the relevant procedural requirement, that is a threshold defense. not a billing defense, but a procedural one.

On framing: the initial claim number is often inclusive of contested territory. items the tenant's advisor believes might be errors but hasn't confirmed. A property manager who responds with organized documentation that addresses the most likely confirmed errors reduces the scope before formal negotiations begin.

The letter is not the beginning of a dispute. It is the formalization of one. Preparation that started before the letter arrives changes what happens after.
