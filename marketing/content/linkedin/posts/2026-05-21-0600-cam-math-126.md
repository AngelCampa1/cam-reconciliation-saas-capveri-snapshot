Every tenant auditor starts with the same exercise: compare this year's CAM charges to last year's. Any line item that jumped materially gets flagged for investigation.

If you haven't already identified and documented those variances before the statement goes out, you're handing the auditor their roadmap.

The threshold for a meaningful variance is both a percentage and a dollar test. A 50% increase on a $200 line item is noise. A 12% increase on an $80,000 line item is $9,600. that warrants investigation before it reaches a tenant.

The HVAC maintenance line is where most classification errors hide. A 55% year-over-year increase almost always signals a capital project (compressor replacement, rooftop unit overhaul) that was coded to operating maintenance rather than CapEx. That one misclassified invoice inflates the entire CAM pool and produces an immediate audit finding.

The variance memo is not optional bureaucracy. It serves three purposes: internal QA before delivery, ready-made answers when tenants ask questions, and audit defense when a formal request arrives. A well-documented variance memo turns a four-hour tenant inquiry response into a 15-minute email with attachments.

Per-SF analysis matters when occupancy changed between years. A janitorial line that went from $156,000 to $174,200 (11.7%) while occupancy increased from 82% to 91% might look flat on a per-occupied-SF basis. The dollar increase was driven by serving more space, not by cost inflation. Without the per-SF context, the tenant sees a 12% increase with no explanation.

Run the variance analysis before the reconciliation goes out. Not after the tenant calls.
