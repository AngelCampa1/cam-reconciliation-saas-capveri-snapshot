For a retail property with $3.5M in operating expenses, running at 2.5% leakage. the midpoint of the illustrative retail leakage range below. the under-recovery is $87,500 per year. Over three years, with no tenant complaint, that's $262,500 the owner was owed and never billed.

The most expensive CAM problem is not overbilling. It is the chronic under-recovery that compounds for years before anyone checks.

CAM leakage. expenses the lease permits to be billed that are not being billed. runs at predictably different rates by property type. Based on industry estimates derived from tenant audit firm published findings and operating-expense benchmarks, illustrative annual leakage ranges by property type: retail 1.5-3.5%, office 1.0-2.5%, industrial 0.5-1.5%, medical office 2.0-3.5%.

Medical office buildings sit at the high end of that range. The categorization between medical-specific and standard office components in MOB leases is complex enough that classification errors in the landlord's favor are common.

These percentages translate to real numbers. A retail property with $3.5M in operating expenses and 2.5% leakage is under-recovering $87,500 per year. At a 6% cap rate, the owner is suppressing $1.46M in asset value annually. not from market conditions, but from a reconciliation that doesn't reflect what the lease permits.

The counterintuitive part of leakage is that it often looks like operational efficiency. The recovery ratio is below average, but the property is also below benchmark on total expenses, so nothing flags as obviously wrong. The problem only surfaces when someone compares the pool to the lease definition and finds recoverable items that were never billed.

Leakage and overbilling often coexist in the same portfolio. A building can be overbilling on gross-up while under-recovering on expense categorization. The net effect on the recovery ratio may look unremarkable.

Separate investigations are required.
