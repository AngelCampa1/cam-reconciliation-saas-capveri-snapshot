A 20-building portfolio delivering CAM statements 90 days late is sitting on $300,000 in delayed receivables, and extending every tenant's audit window by three months.

The cash flow impact is the obvious one: a 20-building portfolio with $15,000 average true-ups has $300,000 in delayed receivables for every month late. At 5% cost of capital, that's real carrying cost.

The less obvious impact: every day you delay delivery, the tenant's audit window extends.

If the lease gives the tenant 120 days to initiate an audit after receiving the statement, and you send the statement 90 days late, you've extended your audit exposure by three months. That's three additional months during which the tenant can retain an auditor, the auditor has more time to be thorough, and you're fielding questions during Q4 when everyone is closing the books again.

Some leases go further. Forfeiture clauses that read "If Landlord fails to deliver the Annual Statement within 180 days of year-end, Landlord shall forfeit the right to collect any additional amounts for that year" are increasingly common in tenant-favorable markets. A late statement becomes a zero true-up.

And the California layer: SB 1103 allows qualifying small-business tenants to request documentation. The landlord has 30 days to respond. If the reconciliation hasn't been sent yet, you can't respond to documentation requests about it. Miss the 30-day window and treble damages apply.

Tenants who receive their reconciliation statement on time, with a clear cover letter explaining material changes, rarely audit. Tenants who receive a statement six months late call their broker. The broker recommends an audit.

The delay didn't cause the errors. It created the adversarial dynamic that turns errors into disputes.
