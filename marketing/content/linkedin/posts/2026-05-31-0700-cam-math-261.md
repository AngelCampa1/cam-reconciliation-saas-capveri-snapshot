The following is an illustrative scenario based on a common GL-level coding error pattern in Class A office buildings:

The reconciliation had been running for six years without a tenant dispute. A Class A office building, 180,000 SF, 12 tenants, annual statements out by April. The controller had a system that worked.

In February 2025, a mid-size professional services firm received the 2024 CAM statement. The year-over-year increase was 11.8% notable but not alarming, given the prior three years had averaged 4.2% annually. The tenant's in-house finance team noted it and sent a question: "Can you explain the primary drivers of the increase this year?"

A reasonable question. The controller didn't have a prepared answer.

The process of building the explanation took three days. The janitorial line had increased 47% year over year. from $162,000 to $238,000. That single line drove most of the total increase. The controller had to trace back through the GL to find that a new janitorial contract had been implemented mid-year with extended overnight service on two floors, and that the prior year had included a vendor credit that reduced the 2023 total by $18,000. The 2024 baseline was $162,000 but the "real" prior year run rate without the credit was $180,000, making the actual increase 32% still high, but from a different baseline.

The controller also found, while building the explanation, that the janitorial GL account included $24,000 in equipment rental for floor machines that had been purchased outright in Q4 2023. The Q4 purchase was capital. The Q4 rental charges in 2024 were opex. But the transition coding had left $24,000 in the account that shouldn't have been there.

The variance analysis the controller built for the tenant conversation became a more significant document than the cover letter it was supposed to support: it found a $24,000 coding error that produced an $8,640 credit to the building's tenants and a revised reconciliation statement.

The variance memo should be built before the statement goes out. Not for tenant relations. though it helps. For the same reason any QA step is built into a process: because it catches errors that otherwise survive until an external party finds them.

A line item that jumped 47% year over year and wasn't investigated before the statement was sent is a potential dispute waiting on the tenant's review calendar.
