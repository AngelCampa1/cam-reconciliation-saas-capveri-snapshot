Most property accounting teams still rely on Excel for at least part of their CAM reconciliation process. This is not because they have not heard of purpose-built software. It is because the spreadsheet has real advantages: flexibility, familiarity, zero marginal cost, and an existing workbook that works.

The problem is not that Excel is bad. The problem is that its failure modes have become expensive.

What changed:

Tenant audit firms got better. Five years ago, tenant auditors reviewed statement totals. Now they request the workbook and check the formulas. They know the common Excel errors. broken SUM ranges, hardcoded overrides, stale cell references. And They look for them specifically.

Portfolio sizes grew. A controller managing five properties can maintain five spreadsheets. A controller managing 25 has 25 spreadsheets, each slightly different, each updated once a year, each with its own formula structure. The probability of an error in at least one workbook approaches certainty.

Expenses grew. The dollar value of CAM pass-throughs increased. The same percentage error creates a larger dollar finding because the pool is larger.

ASC 842 raised documentation standards. Tenant CFOs and their external auditors scrutinize CAM charges as part of their lease accounting process. "Trust my spreadsheet" is not sufficient documentation for an external audit.

The specific risks that matter most: silent formula errors that look reasonable (no audit trail, so they persist for years), version control collapse during Q1 crunch (three controllers, 30 spreadsheets, no single source of truth), and key-person dependency (the workbook works because one person understands its structure; when that person leaves, the knowledge leaves with them).

The math on risk versus cost: a purpose-built CAM tool costs $200-$500 per property per year. A 10% annual probability of a material spreadsheet error, at $50,000 average cost, is a $5,000 expected annual loss per property. The tool costs less than the expected loss.

The transition does not require an all-or-nothing switch. Phase 1 is adding a validation layer. keep using Excel but run the output through a tool that checks for common errors before statements go out. Phase 2 is a parallel run: calculate in both Excel and the new system for one cycle, compare results, build confidence. Phase 3 is making the new system the record.

The spreadsheet is not dying because it is bad. It is dying because the cost of its failure modes now exceeds the cost of the alternatives.
