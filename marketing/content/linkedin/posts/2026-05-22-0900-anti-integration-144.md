The wrong way to build a business case for CAM reconciliation software: "We need better tools."

This sounds like a quality-of-life request. It gets a polite nod and no budget.

The right frame: three numbers in terms the CFO already cares about.

First, additional recovery. For a 20-property office portfolio with $38M in recoverable expenses and an 87% recovery ratio, the gap to an 92% benchmark is $1.9M per year. Software captures 25-40% of that gap by finding billing errors, unmapped GL accounts, and gross-up miscalculations. Conservative capture: $475,000-$760,000 annually.

Second, reduced audit exposure. Using conservative modeled assumptions for a 30-property portfolio: eight tenant audit requests per year. A 65% finding rate. An average finding of $35,000. Settlement at 60%. That models out to approximately $109,200 in annual audit exposure before internal staff time. If software catches errors before statements go out, the finding rate drops from 65% to 20%. Audit cost savings: $75,600-$87,600 per year.

Third, controller capacity. Twenty properties at 12-25 hours saved per property equals 240-500 hours redirected to lease analysis, ownership reporting, and acquisition support instead of copying formulas.

Total annual benefit: $568,600-$885,100. Software cost for a validation tool: $4,000-$10,000 per year.

When the CFO asks "what happens if we don't?" the answer is: current annual audit exposure of $109,200, undetected errors compounding over the lookback period, and controller hours spent on manual calculations instead of analysis.

The business case proves itself. Run one property through the software. If it finds errors worth more than the annual subscription, the decision is made.
