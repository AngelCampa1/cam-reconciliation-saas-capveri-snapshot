A single formula cell - =SUM(A12:A43,A45:A52) instead of =SUM(A12:A52) - compounds into $247,000 in refunds on a 180,000 SF building over three reconciliation cycles.

The building is 180,000 square feet. Fourteen tenants. One reconciliation spreadsheet inherited from the previous controller.

The formula =SUM(A12:A52) should capture the full insurance pool. Someone typed =SUM(A12:A43,A45:A52). Cell A44, a $142,000 annual insurance premium. was excluded from the sum.

In year one, the recovery ratio drops from 91% to 84%. The controller looks at it, notices the gap, and draws the obvious wrong conclusion: occupancy is driving the shortfall, and the building needs to recover more through the gross-up mechanism. So the insurance gross-up factor gets adjusted upward.

That adjustment is the second error. Insurance is a fixed expense. It does not vary with occupancy. Grossing it up is a lease violation. But it looks like a correction. the recovery ratio improves, and the controller doesn't know why it was off in the first place.

For three years, the insurance premium is missing from the base pool and the gross-up is applied to insurance at a rate it should never see. The overbilling runs approximately $82,000 per year.

Year three: a tenant whose lease is expiring hires an audit firm as part of their exit review. The auditor finds both errors in 30 minutes. the missing cell and the gross-up on a fixed expense. And Traces them back to year one.

Refunds total $247,000. Five other tenants retain the same firm when they hear what was found. The largest tenant, 23% of the building, does not renew.

The recovery ratio variance was the signal. A 7-point drop without a corresponding lease change or vacancy explanation is not a market condition. It is evidence that something is wrong with the calculation. Investigating that signal in year one costs an hour. Discovering the cause in year three costs $247,000 in refunds plus the lease and relationship consequences that follow.

The formula was wrong. The gross-up was a response to not knowing the formula was wrong. Both errors were invisible in the billing system because the billing system runs whatever inputs it receives.

The check that catches both is a single step: verify that the CAM pool total on the statement matches the GL, and verify that the gross-up applies to variable expenses only.
