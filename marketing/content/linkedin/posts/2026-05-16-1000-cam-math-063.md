The year-end CAM true-up is the simplest calculation in the reconciliation process: actual obligation minus estimates paid equals the settlement. Getting it wrong is harder than it sounds.

Here's the framework for running clean true-ups.

Step 1: Close the books first. The true-up cannot be accurate until the GL is final. Year-end accruals, December vendor invoices, and fourth-quarter property tax installments must all be posted before the reconciliation runs. Starting the true-up before the GL is final produces a settlement that changes when the last invoice arrives. That corrected statement restarts the tenant's review clock and signals that your process isn't controlled.

Step 2: Verify each tenant's estimate total.
Pull every monthly estimate payment from the tenant ledger. Verify the total against the lease's estimate amounts and any mid-year adjustments. Tenants who received a mid-year estimate increase should have paid a different amount starting at the effective date. Applying the old monthly amount to the full 12 months understates what was collected.

Step 3: Calculate the actual annual obligation first.
Run the full reconciliation: expense pool verification, exclusions, gross-up, pro-rata share, cap calculation, admin fee. The true-up amount is the last calculation, not the first.

Step 4: Apply the cap before calculating the true-up. If the tenant has a controllable expense cap, the cap ceiling applies to the reconciled expense amount. The true-up is based on what the tenant actually owes under the cap, not the gross actual expenses. Settling the true-up at the uncapped number and applying the cap afterward is the same math error twice.

Step 5: Issue credits and charges with payment terms. A positive true-up (tenant owes the landlord) is a bill with a due date. A negative true-up (landlord owes the tenant) is a credit applied against the next month's rent, or a refund. the lease specifies which. Credit tenants who are never paid erode the landlord-tenant relationship faster than any billing dispute.

The example that illustrates the stakes: a tenant whose monthly estimate was $21,250 ($255,000 for the year) and whose actual obligation was $266,637 owes a true-up of $11,637. That's a well-run reconciliation. estimates were accurate to within 4.6%, the true-up is less than one month's estimate, and there's very little for a tenant auditor to dispute.

Miss the statement delivery deadline and that $11,637 may be uncollectable.
