CapEx misclassification is the highest-dollar single finding in tenant CAM audits. Not because it's hard to catch. Because no one catches it before the statement goes out.

A roof replacement coded to HVAC maintenance. A parking lot resurfacing coded to repairs. An elevator modernization coded to building operations. Each one is a capital expenditure sitting in the operating expense pool.

The dollar impact: a $150,000 roof replacement in the CAM pool, on a 10-tenant building with 10% average pro-rata shares, produces $15,000 in overbilling per tenant. For three tenants with 36-month audit windows who all exercise their rights in the same quarter: $45,000 in refunds from one miscoded invoice.

The classification test that matters: does this cost create a new asset, substantially upgrade an existing one, or extend its useful life? If yes. it's capital. If it restores existing functionality without bettering or extending. it's a repair.

This isn't tax advice. It's a classification discipline. Borderline items need a classification memo that documents why the item was treated as OpEx rather than CapEx. The memo is what survives an audit. Without it, the amount is treated as CapEx by default and removed from the pool.

Pre-send GL review, five minutes: pull every transaction over $10,000 in operating maintenance accounts. For each one, ask whether the description says "replacement," "installation," "upgrade," or "system." Any of those words is a capital signal.

Catch it before the statement. The tenant's auditor will catch it after.
