Tenant audit firms have grown significantly since 2020. That number is not random.

Three things drove it. FASB ASC 842 put variable CAM charges on tenant balance sheets, which meant tenants' auditors started looking at CAM statements as part of routine year-end review. SB 1103 in California raised the financial upside of finding errors for small-business tenants. Vacancy pressure also gave larger tenants more room to demand audit rights and then exercise them.

The business model works on contingency. Audit firms typically take 25-40% of recoveries, with experienced repeat tenants negotiating closer to 20-25%. On a $100,000 finding, the firm earns roughly $25,000-$40,000. That fee structure means they only bring cases they expect to win, which means the majority finding rate reported in the industry is not inflated by low-confidence attempts. When they come, they've already done the preliminary work.

What changed in the audit methodology is the use of technology. Auditors who used to manually abstract leases and trace GL accounts now run automated comparisons. The cost of doing a CAM audit dropped. The margin on each engagement improved. So firms are taking more tenants and smaller properties than they used to.

For property managers, the implication is straightforward: the audit firm your tenant hired has likely already identified the specific errors before sending the engagement notice. The first letter is not an opening question. It is the beginning of a documented resolution process.

Knowing what they check first is the relevant preparation.
