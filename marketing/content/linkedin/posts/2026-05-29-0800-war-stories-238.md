CAM leakage is the mirror image of overbilling. It is the money the lease allows a landlord to recover that the landlord is not recovering.

Unlike overbilling, CAM leakage does not generate demand letters or audit claims. It generates nothing except a recovery ratio that is lower than it should be. The cost compounds silently until someone compares the reconciliation to what the lease actually permits.

Here is a scenario that represents a pattern seen across aging portfolios.

A 12-property suburban office portfolio has been under the same property management firm for six years. The reconciliation process runs from a template built at the time the management contract was signed. The template has not been updated to reflect lease amendments.

In year four, several leases renew with amended gross-up thresholds. The original leases had a 90% occupancy threshold for gross-up. The renewals moved to 85%. The template still uses 90%.

At current occupancy of 87%, the template suppresses the gross-up entirely because 87% is below 90%. Under the amended leases, 87% is above 85%, so the gross-up should be applied. The variable expenses. approximately $400,000 per year across the affected buildings. are being billed without the 1.03x multiplier that the leases now allow.

The annual leakage from this single template error: approximately $12,000 per year per affected building. Across six buildings with amended leases, $72,000 per year. Over three years since the amendments: $216,000 in recoverable income that was not recovered.

The template error was never malicious. It was an omission that resulted from not having a process for updating the reconciliation configuration when leases are amended.

There are five types of leakage, and the gross-up misconfiguration described here is one of them. The other four. cap escalator tracking errors, pro-rata denominator errors, expense classification omissions, and base year drift. all share the same root cause: the reconciliation process was not designed to stay current with lease terms as they change.

The correction requires comparing the current reconciliation output to what the lease permits. That comparison is the annual check that prevents leakage from compounding.
